“Perhaps the responsibility we inherit is not simply to preserve the memory of history, but to preserve the humanity within it.”Jesmond Saliba

Different histories, but a shared recognition of the human cost of war.

For Malta, remembrance carries particular significance. Our islands endured relentless bombardment, destruction, hunger and loss during the Second World War. The George Cross remains a reminder of the extraordinary courage and resilience shown by the Maltese people.

This is why the ceremonies held each November matter. From the solemn commemoration at St John's Co-Cathedral to the laying of wreaths and military honours at the War Memorial in Floriana, they connect our present with a history that must never become distant or abstract.

But remembrance must also extend beyond ceremony.

The Royal British Legion, through its longstanding presence in Malta and its wider work, embodies an important principle: remembering those who died must go hand in hand with caring for those who served, those who survived and their families.

Its volunteers, its Poppy Appeal and its continuing commitment to welfare demonstrate how remembrance becomes an act of solidarity.

As Commissioner for Voluntary Organisations, this dimension is particularly meaningful to me. Voluntary service is one of the ways a society transforms gratitude into responsibility, and remembrance into practical care for others.

There is another dimension to remembrance that I find increasingly important.

History is often told through events, dates, battles and the decisions of those who led them. Yet history was lived by people. Behind every name engraved on a memorial was a human life, with relationships, aspirations and a future that may never have been realised.

Perhaps this is why the poppy remains such a powerful symbol. It brings the human being back into a story that can otherwise become dominated by institutions, armies and nations.

And perhaps the greatest responsibility of remembrance is precisely this: to ensure that, even as generations pass and the world changes, we never lose sight of the people behind the history.

Looking again at that small pin, and at the image of poppies falling over Floriana, I see a connection between generations. Between the lessons we learned as children, the sacrifices of those who came before us and the responsibilities we now carry.

As fewer people remain who experienced the Second World War firsthand, we have a duty to pass on more than dates, ceremonies and symbols. We must help younger generations understand why remembrance matters, why peace cannot be taken for granted and why human dignity must remain at the heart of our collective memory.

The poppy reminds us of those who gave their lives. The work of organisations such as the Royal British Legion reminds us of those who continue to live with the consequences.

Perhaps the responsibility we inherit is not simply to preserve the memory of history, but to preserve the humanity within it.

Because remembrance is ultimately about people. And so is the future we have a responsibility to build.

Lest We Forget.

Jesmond Saliba
