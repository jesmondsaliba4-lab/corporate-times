The budget

Budget 2027, presented on 26 October, raised the price of that fuel. It asked non-EU workers, visitors and non-residents to pay more. Higher permit fees and the withdrawal of some benefits for recent arrivals make it more expensive to recruit from outside the Union. Proposals for international students go further: the long-stay study visa would rise from €100 to €300, or from €150 to €400 for the extended service; the first study residence permit from €50 to €500; and a new annual €400 infrastructure contribution would be added.

The intention is clear and, in principle, widely shared: to move the economy towards higher productivity. The question November kept returning to is whether raising the cost of people, on its own, produces it.

The employers

The bluntest answer came from Malta Employers on 2 November. Growth in recent years, the association said, relied heavily on sustained construction, encouraged by pro-development policies, which in turn generated hotels, shopping malls and commercial projects — all labour-intensive. “In allowing such development to take place, government knew full well that Malta did not have enough local resources to sustain it and the country would need to resort to foreign workers to fill the gaps.” Deciding the economic model, and planning the infrastructure to carry it, is the government’s job, it argued — not something to be “suddenly transferred to the private sector”.

Others spoke for their sectors. The Association of Maltese International Trailer Operators called for a national strategy for professional drivers. Europe’s road transport sector, it noted, has more than 502,000 unfilled driver positions, a further 660,000 drivers are expected to retire by 2030, and 65% of European trucking companies named driver shortages their primary concern in 2025. Maltese operators have struggled for years to recruit Maltese drivers; their reliance on third-country nationals, ATTO’s chairman Joseph Bugeja said, “has not arisen out of preference”.

The English-language schools, through FELTOM, asked the government to publish the data and economic assessment behind the student fees. International students, they pointed out, also fill rooms, restaurants, buses and shops; extra fee revenue must be weighed against lost enrolments.

And at the Malta Institute of Accountants’ tax conference on 19 November, chief executive Maria Cauchi Delia said the Institute shared the government’s productivity objectives, but that automation and skills development take time. She asked for “a fair, sector-sensitive transition”, with clear guidance, to protect essential expertise, continuity of service and the workers and families affected.
